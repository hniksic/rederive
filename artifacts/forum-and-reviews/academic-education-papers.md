# Academic / math-education literature on Derive in the classroom

## Roanes-Lozano, Galán-García & Solano-Macías (2019) - "Some Reflections
## About the Success and Impact of the Computer Algebra System DERIVE
## with a 10-Year Time Perspective"

Sources:
- https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11786-019-00404-9 (paywalled,
  abstract only)
- https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334232563 (abstract only,
  full PDF not retrievable in this session)
- https://docta.ucm.es/handle/20.500.14352/13776 (institutional repository
  entry, abstract only)

Published in *Mathematics in Computer Science*, vol. 13, issue 3 (2019),
pp. 417-431.

Abstract content recovered:

> "DERIVE had a very important impact in teaching mathematics with
> technology, mainly in the 1990's." The authors analyze the possible
> reasons for its success and impact and give personal conclusions based
> on the facts collected. "More than 10 years after it was discontinued
> it is still used for teaching and several scientific papers (most
> devoted to educational issues) still refer to it."

This is arguably the single most authoritative retrospective academic
source on *why* Derive succeeded and what its legacy was, written by
authors embedded in the Spanish math-education community (one of the
strongest Derive adoption markets in Europe). Full text was not
accessible in this session (Springer paywall; ResearchGate/UCM repository
only exposed the abstract) - **recommended follow-up**: obtain full text
via institutional access or interlibrary loan, since the "personal
conclusions" section almost certainly contains exactly the kind of
firsthand classroom-reception anecdotes this research task is looking
for.

## Pierce & Stacey (2001) - "Observations on Students' Responses to
## Learning in a CAS Environment"

Source: https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ635963
Published in *Mathematics Education Research Journal*, Vol. 13, No. 1,
April 2001, pp. 28-46. ERIC #EJ635963.

Abstract content recovered:

> Reports on undergraduate students' responses while using DERIVE in an
> introductory calculus course. The technology acted as a "catalyst for
> students to use three types of learning strategies": (1) regularly
> using multiple representations, (2) discussing mathematical meaning
> with peers and instructors, and (3) incorporating the computer into
> collaborative discussion/problem-solving.

Useful as evidence for a specific, often-cited claim about *why* Derive
mattered pedagogically: not just "it computes answers" but that it
changed classroom social dynamics - students talking to each other and to
instructors about what an expression *meant*, prompted by having a CAS in
the room. This is a recurring theme across the CAS-education literature
of the era (also echoed, less formally, in the Scientific Computing World
review's discussion of Derive 6's "Display Steps" feature - see
`magazine-reviews.md`).

  dismissed as "just a teaching toy."

## Chartwell-Yorke curated review quotes (marketing page, but sourced
## from real named UK education press)

Source: https://www.chartwellyorke.com/derive/derivereviews.html

This is a vendor's own testimonials page (Chartwell-Yorke was Derive's UK
distributor), so treat the selection as favorably curated, but the quoted
outlets and quotes themselves appear to be genuine excerpts from real UK
educational-technology trade press:

- **Evaluate**: "An extremely powerful tool with extensive
  functionality... work on balancing equations could not have been
  replicated using more mainstream programs."
- **Schoolzone**: "A brilliant tool for preparing worksheets... an
  impressive piece of software."
- **TEEM** (Teachers Evaluating Educational Multimedia): "Derive 6 is an
  excellent tool for mathematics students and teachers at Key Stage 4 and
  above... an ideal tool for students supported by teachers and
  independent learners."
- **Scientific Computing World** (March/April 2004): "Too good just for
  students" (see `magazine-reviews.md` for the full article this
  headline is drawn from).
- Education Guardian review also referenced (as a scanned image on the
  page) but its text was not extractable via automated tooling.

These UK-specific outlets (TEEM, Schoolzone, Evaluate) are themselves
evidence of just how embedded Derive was in British secondary-education
IT-evaluation infrastructure by the early-to-mid 2000s - there was a whole
cottage industry of UK bodies whose job was rating classroom software,
and Derive was a fixture in that world.
