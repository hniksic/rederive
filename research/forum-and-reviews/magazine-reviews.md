# Magazine reviews of Derive and its muMATH ancestor

## Scientific Computing World - "Derive 6: Far too good just for students"

Source: https://www.scientific-computing.com/feature/derive-6-far-too-good-just-students
Byline/date: reviewed in the March/April 2004 issue of Scientific Computing
World (per Chartwell-Yorke's own reviews page, which cites this piece by
that issue date).

Full detail recovered from the article:

- Opens with a historical anecdote: Frederick Nelson Cole's famous 1903
  AMS presentation, where factoring a Mersenne number by hand took him
  "20 years of Sunday work." The reviewer's punchline: "With Derive 6 on
  a Pentium, the equivalent FACTOR(2^67-1, Rational) takes 0.281
  seconds." Used to frame just how far symbolic computation had come.
- Traces Derive's roots to Soft Warehouse in Hawaii, founded on minimal
  resources: "4k RAM, and $1,000 working capital." Notes Texas
  Instruments acquired the program in 1999 and integrated it into their
  graphing-calculator line.
- Praises the educational focus of v6: "Display Steps" (shows the
  transformation rules applied during simplification, so students can
  follow *how* an answer was reached, not just see the answer) and
  slider-bar controls for animating graph parameters - explicitly framed
  as addressing the pedagogical worry that CAS software lets students
  skip understanding.
- Praises the manual's "educators' footnotes" written by CAS-education
  researchers, and UI/graphics upgrades: automatic plot labeling, 3D
  mouse-rotation, Unicode font support "unifying all expressions and text,
  whatever the language."
- Praises cross-compatibility: worksheets can move between the PC version
  and TI graphing calculators, though "syntax differences" mean some
  editing is needed to make a worksheet run correctly on both.
- Main criticism: discoverability. "Most functions aren't accessible via
  the front menu" - e.g., finding the Gamma function requires digging
  into the help files rather than browsing an obvious menu, which the
  reviewer calls a usability gap versus competing software.
- On raw power: narrower than Maple or Mathematica, but "closely
  comparable in power" to the cut-down "Mathematica Lite"
  (Wolfram CalculationCenter) product of the day.
- Verdict: "a solid 'evolutionary' update," and the reviewer explicitly
  argues it is "far too good to be limited to the educational field" -
  i.e. recommends it for serious professional use, not just classrooms
  (hence the article's title).

## BYTE Magazine, November 1980 - muSIMP/muMATH-79 review

Citation (per Wikipedia's muMATH article bibliography):
Gregg Williams, "The muSIMP/muMATH-79 Symbolic Math system, a Review,"
BYTE, November 1980, p. 324.

Issue location on the Internet Archive:
https://archive.org/details/byte-magazine-1980-11
(full-text OCR also at https://archive.org/details/eu_BYTE-1980-11_OCR)

Note: the review text itself is buried around page 324 of a
~450-page scanned issue. Automated full-text fetch tools available in
this research session could only retrieve the opening table-of-contents
pages of the OCR transcript, not the specific review page, and the
Archive's page-level full-text-search API returned "no OCR/hOCR data
present" for this item. The citation is solid (confirmed independently
via Wikipedia's muMATH article and via a Creative Computing bibliography
reference to it) but the review's actual prose could not be quoted
directly in this session. Recommended follow-up: open the item's reader
UI directly and jump to page ~324, or download the PDF and search
locally.

This is the earliest known independent product review of the Soft
Warehouse lineage that would become Derive - i.e., the ancestor-software
review that establishes the brand's original reputation ("symbolic math
on a microcomputer, that's affordable") three generations of hardware
before Derive itself shipped.

## Creative Computing Magazine, October 1982 - muSIMP/muMATH-80 review

Citation: David D. Shochat, "A Symbolic Mathematics System" (revision of
an earlier review that ran in SIGPC Notes), Creative Computing, October
1982, p. 26.

Full text location:
https://archive.org/stream/creativecomputing-1982-10/Creative_Computing_v08_n10_1982_October_djvu.txt

Recovered details:
- Product covered: muSimp/muMath-80, for CP/M, Apple II, and TRS-80 (48K
  required), priced $250, distributed at the time by Microsoft.
- The review opens with the reviewer's personal framing: roughly 20 years
  earlier he had learned that digital computers could do numerical
  differentiation/integration, but that symbolic manipulation of
  expressions was assumed to be outside a computer's reach - i.e., the
  review is written explicitly to convey "this shouldn't be possible on a
  home computer, and yet here it is."
- (The full review body past the opening framing was not recoverable via
  automated fetch in this session - the djvu.txt transcript is very long
  and the fetch tool truncated before reaching the review's substantive
  middle/ending. Worth a manual follow-up read of the linked transcript.)

## Desktop Engineering Magazine - Barry Simon's comparative CAS reviews

Citations (located via bibliography references, full text not freely
available online / not digitized on archive.org as of this research):

- Barry Simon, "Symbolic Math Powerhouses," Desktop Engineering, Vol. 1,
  Issue 1, September/October 1995, pp. 42-51.
- Barry Simon, "Symbolic Math Powerhouses Revisited," Desktop Engineering,
  Vol. 2, Issue 7, March 1997, pp. 12-25, 52-53.

These are two rounds (1995 and 1997) of the same reviewer doing a
head-to-head shootout of Derive, Mathematica, Maple, and other symbolic
packages of the day for an engineering-trade audience - i.e. exactly the
kind of "which CAS should I buy" buyer's-guide piece PC Magazine/BYTE ran
for other software categories. Full text not accessible online during
this research session (Desktop Engineering's archives from that era are
not on archive.org and are not otherwise indexed); flagged here as a
known, named, dated primary source worth chasing down via a university
library microfiche/periodicals collection if a print-quality quote is
needed for the requirements document.

## A Critique of the Mathematical Abilities of CA Systems (Wester)

Source: https://math.unm.edu/~wester/cas_review.html
Context: companion web page to Michael J. Wester (ed.), *Computer Algebra
Systems: A Practical Guide*, Wiley, 1999.

This is a rigorous, test-suite-driven comparison (not a magazine review)
of Axiom, Derive, Macsyma, Maple, Mathematica, MuPAD, and Reduce across
~27 categories of mathematical problems (Boolean logic, calculus, ODEs,
limits, integration, etc.), with individual per-system problem-set files
linked. The page itself is an index/table of contents; the actual
comparative critique is in a linked 40-page PostScript/PDF document that
could not be retrieved in full during this session. Notable as evidence
that Derive was taken seriously enough in the mid-to-late 1990s to be
benchmarked test-by-test against the "big three" (Maple/Mathematica/
Macsyma) rather than dismissed as merely a toy or teaching aid.
